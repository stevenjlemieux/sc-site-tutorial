The Inverted Transcendentalism of Ahab

While meditating on the woods and the water it is possible to slip out of one’s body and become one with all around. It is possible to transcend one’s physical and mental self and be a church unto one, using nature as a mirror for the soul. Melville paints a picture of just how deadly such transcendence can be. He warns to never look too deeply into nature “lest it invert thee, deaden thee” (Melville 328), yet warnings can be given and warnings can be ignored. Ahab is such an ignorer. In a perversion of Emerson’s idea of transcendence through belief in the divinity of man and the prudent reflection of such in nature, an idea seen through the thoughts of Ishmael, Ahab sees in nature a reflection of all the evils of man, and in doing so becomes mad in his transcendence. Ahab—inverted and deadened, consumed by his monomania—loses himself forever amongst the very essence of nature that he sought to grasp.

Emerson recognizes that nature can reflect a man’s soul. In order to see nature as a mirror and guide one must reach an ecstasy or “become a transparent eyeball” so as to “[be] nothing” yet “see all” (Emerson 6). In such a state Ishmael is able to “lose his identity” (Melville 136) and transcend his single being, becoming part of an Oversoul. To let one’s self transcend too far into “that rocking life imparted by” the sea is perilous, for should “you slip your hold at all… you [shall] drop through that transparent air,” to be lost forever (Melville 136). Ishmael heeds his own warning; he only transcends so far, and as such only “experiences a temporary loss of the sense of individual identity” (McSweeny 94) and is able to regain his worldly self.

Ahab transcends in a different fashion. His is not the ecstasy of the tranquil, but that of the violent, that of a “raving lunatic” (Melville 176): “in a straight jacket, he [swings] to the mad rocking of the gales” (Melville 157). He swings alone. No benign Oversoul to envelope him, he and his “madness of illumination” (Austin 345) are trapped in a nature he sees as the opposite of the serenity Ishmael experiences. Nature is not the reflection of an intrinsic divinity but of a “cosmic evil” (McSweeny 93). Ahab is unable to escape his madness and it lives on even after he has regained his outward composure. And still his “inner self raved on” (Melville 157) in a “demonically introverted transcendentalism“(McSweeny 93).

The eddies of nature, swirling round a man, can lift a man’s soul from his body, and cast his reflection before his eyes. These do naught but lift a prudent man only so far out of his worldly frame. There is a different side of nature though; no longer light eddies, but brutally violent cyclones that have the power to rip a man’s soul from him. Ishmael, whose soul was before set to transcendence by the all empowering harmony of nature is now forced into transcendence by the hell that man can create on earth. Ishmael does not achieve transcendence through his own reflection, but instead is consumed by the “artificial fire” of Ahab’s. “The Pequod” in all of its hideous, hellish glory becomes “the material counterpart of her monomaniac commander’s soul” (Melville 327). And Ishmael pulls away from this Ahab induced transcendence, warning “give not thyself up, then, to fire, lest it invert thee, deaden thee” (Melville 328).

This warning is especially prudent looked at in relation to Ahab, who, already, is turning inward. Ahab, though, flings himself into the fire. Body and soul he hurls himself into the storm. He does not merely transcend, seeing his soul in nature—no, he draws himself into nature and nature into him as he calls to the storm “Oh, thou foundling fire… Leap! leap up, and lick the sky! I leap with thee; I burn with thee; would fain be welded with thee; defyingly I worship thee!” (Melville 383). He worships the storm as he worships himself, for the storm is, like the demonic Pequod, Ahab, “Old Thunder” (Melville 380). As Ahab flies into the fire, against the warnings of Ishmael, he is wholly lost to the obsession of Moby Dick for “heart, soul, and body, lungs and life, old Ahab is bound” (Melville 383) to the pursuit of the white whale. “There is a wisdom that is woe; but there is a woe that is madness” (Melville 328), a madness that Ahab fully succumbs to, as he can no longer see the good of evil. For evil, too, has its place in the world; what is joy without sorrow? Ahab desires no such compromise—he sees evil as God’s betrayal of man, and as such is unacceptable—and is consumed with hatred for this malignant God. He hurls himself ever forward into the flame of nature that is but a reflection of himself; he hurls himself towards Moby Dick.

Moby Dick, the white whale, the most majestic creature of the sea, has heaped upon him “all that most maddens and torments; all that stirs up the lees of things; all truth with malice in it; all that cracks the sinews and cakes the brain; all the subtle demonisms of life and thought; all evil, to crazy Ahab” (Melville 156). It is clear now why Ahab transcends towards evil and madness; Moby Dick is what he meditates upon. Moby Dick is, to Ahab, nature. And with this as his starting point he only can go deeper and deeper into himself until “his fixed will does not allow him to hold in mind a compensatory idea about either the whale or nature” (White 149). As Ahab flies further into himself all that he finds is the evil he hates. With each step he takes inward he takes a step closer to the whale. Ishmael is safe from this fate, for unlike Ahab, Ishmael “succeeds [in forming compensatory opinions on the whale] in his meditations” (White 149). Ishmael is unable to see himself in the whale, in fact “the Albino whale was [to him] the symbol” of nothingness (Melville 165). The thought that Moby Dick could embody nothingness is preposterous to Ishmael, and it is this thought that spurs him to join the “fiery hunt” (Melville 149) not to avenge mankind but to see if there is anything behind the mask.

Ahab, wholly within himself, wholly given to the flame, wholly in pursuit of Moby Dick will not stop until he “[strikes] through the mask” (Melville 140). Like Narcissus he seeks to “grasp the tormenting, mild image… [that] is the image of the ungraspable phantom of life” (Melville 20), for “Ahab, too, is possessed by [this] phantom” (Austin 347). To go against nature as such, to attempt to grasp “the ungraspable phantom” is a perversion of Emerson’s ideal “of the active soul seeking harmony with the forces that created him” (White 147). He swells himself with so great an egotism that he believes none are higher than he, and he would “strike the sun if it insulted [him]” (Melville 140). An egotism so great, mixed with his monomania and madness are what “[lead] to his [own] destruction and that of his ship” (White 147). Like Narcissus who “plunged into [the fountain] and was drowned” (Melville 20), so too went Ahab. He is finally face to face with Moby Dick, with his reflection. Not content with simply seeing Moby Dick as the “pasteboard mask,” just as Narcissus was not content with the “mild image;” Ahab strives to go further, to dive in deeper. He “strikes through the mask” as his counterpart dove into the fountain. Both are acts of their undoing. With his harpoon plunged into Moby Dick, the image within his grasp, Ahab is “shot out of the boat” (Melville 426) and is drowned.

All but one sink with Ahab in his introverted transcendence and mad pursuit of the “one grand hooded phantom” (Melville 22), who was the very essence of nature. Ishmael survives not because he was radically different from Ahab, but instead because he saw the “wisdom of woe” and accepted sorrow as necessary. He shied from the fire and only sought to see the “grand hooded phantom” rather than grasp it. Ishmael survives for the same reason all others perish with Ahab, that “there is a Catskill eagle in some souls that can alike dive down into the blackest gorges, and soar out of them again and become invisible in the sunny spaces” (Melville 328). Both Ahab and Ishmael possessed this higher soul, both could not help but soar high above the heads of all others, for should he “[fly] within the gorge, that gorge is in the mountains.” The crew lies hopelessly trapped in the machinations of Ahab, for they are birds of the plain and fly far below Ahab, unable to escape the tug of his “ungodly, godlike” (Melville 78) soul. Ishmael, too, flies high, yet unlike Ahab—who flew too far and thus became completely inverted and dead to the world—he looks down and becomes one with his surroundings. The connection Ishmael finds within the Oversoul becomes his saving grace. While Ahab drags down the crew of lesser, ground bound men with the all pervasiveness of his soul Ishmael floats in harmony with the savage sea hawks and sharks, in a state of grace Ahab’s monomaniacal soul failed to grasp.
